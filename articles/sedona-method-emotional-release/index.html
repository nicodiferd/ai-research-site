🧘 Four Questions That Release Negative Emotion

The Sedona Method — a deceptively simple framework for processing emotions instead of just expressing them

Instagram @brooksbelieves · 4,088 likes · Apr 9, 2026
⚠️ Note: The Sedona Method originates from Lester Levenson's work in the 1970s. While many practitioners report positive results, the technique lacks controlled clinical studies. This content is wellness-oriented, not clinical. Consult a mental health professional for clinical conditions.

There's a difference between expressing an emotion and releasing it. Most people know this intuitively — you've vented about something a hundred times and still feel the same way. The expression didn't clear it. @brooksbelieves presents the Sedona Method as a framework for the other path: actual release, through four deceptively simple questions asked on repeat.

The Distinction: Expression vs. Release

When you express a negative emotion through negative action — snapping at someone, spiraling in your head, doom-scrolling — you're not getting rid of it. You're practicing it. Each expression embeds the emotion deeper, making it feel more like a permanent part of who you are.

"There's a difference between expressing emotions and releasing emotions. A lot of times we express negative emotions in negative action, but it doesn't actually release it. That's how you make it a part of you — but it's not a part of you at all."

The linguistic framing matters too. English pushes us toward "I am angry" instead of "I feel anger." One makes the emotion your identity. The other recognizes it as a temporary channel passing through you.

The Four Questions

The method itself is a cycle of four questions, repeated 10-20 times while sitting with the feeling:

Question 1
Could I welcome this feeling?

Not "do I want to" — just "could I?" Forces you to actually sit with it.

Question 2
Could I release this feeling?

Introduces the possibility. The answer can be no.

Question 3
Would I?

Shifts from possibility to willingness. Usually yes — you know holding it hurts.

Question 4
When?

Forces a timeline. You won't feel this in a year. So... when?

The key: answers can be yes or no. You're not forcing positivity. If you ask "could I welcome this feeling?" and the answer is "absolutely not, please die" — that's fine. The point is turning the gears. Making the unconscious conscious. Each pass through the cycle deepens your actual contact with the emotion.

Why Repetition Works

The mechanism, as Brooks explains it, is simple: most of us never actually sit with a feeling. We react to it, talk about it, intellectualize it — but we don't welcome it as a direct experience. The four-question cycle forces repeated contact:

The Cycle Effect

Pass 1-3: Resistance. The feeling is still foreign, still something to fight against.

Pass 4-7: Familiarity. You've welcomed it enough times that it stops being a threat.

Pass 8-10: Acceptance. "I know this feeling well now. I just accepted it 10 times."

Breakthrough: "Would I release it? Yes. When? Now." And something lifts.

"Once you welcome the feeling 10 times and actually have to feel it newly 10 times... by the 10th time you're usually like, all right, I know this feeling well now. I just accepted it like 10 times. I can actually release it."

The Identity Layer

The deeper principle beneath the technique is about identification. When you say "I am angry," the emotion becomes structural — load-bearing in your sense of self. When you recognize emotions as channels passing through you, release becomes possible because you're not dismantling your identity to let go.

"You're not any emotion. You're just a channel."

This maps onto several established therapeutic frameworks — ACT's cognitive defusion, IFS's parts work, mindfulness-based non-identification. The Sedona Method packages a similar insight into a repeatable, low-barrier technique that works for everything from major trauma to daily irritation.

How to Try It

  • Start small: Pick a minor current frustration, not your deepest wound
  • Sit with it: Don't intellectualize. Actually feel the feeling in your body
  • Run the four questions: Could I welcome it? Could I release it? Would I? When?
  • Repeat 10-20 times: Each pass should be a fresh encounter with the feeling
  • Don't force: "No" answers are valid and part of the process
  • Scale up gradually: Once you trust the process on small things, apply it to bigger ones

Brooks uses it almost daily for anything that feels constricting. The claim isn't that four questions solve everything — it's that they provide a concrete alternative to the default mode of suppression, distraction, or expression without release.
