🧪 The 3-Prompt Stock Analysis Workflow

Reverse-engineering what @charan.invests is actually doing with Claude — and the reconstructable prompt templates to replicate it yourself

Instagram @charan.invests · ~47s reel · Apr 19, 2026

The reel is a 47-second pitch: the creator claims he uses Claude to find stocks like AAOI and Nebius before they run 100%+, and wants you to join his 15k-member Discord to get the prompts. The content is intentionally thin — the prompts themselves are the paywall.

But the creator narrates the structure of the workflow on camera, and the structure is the actually valuable part. The prompts can be rebuilt from the narration. That's what this page is for.

The workflow is a three-step funnel: breadth → comparison → adversarial. It's the same pattern equity research analysts use, just expressed as prompts. Anyone can reconstruct it — the Discord is the convenience tax, not the IP.

What He Actually Says On Camera

Stripped of the marketing, here's the workflow he describes:

Prompt 1
Deep Dive

Financials, asymmetry, and possible catalysts — outputs a "well-formatted" overview and a score

Prompt 2
Peer Comparison

Compare the target to its peers so the valuation can be sanity-checked ("does this actually make sense?")

Prompt 3
Bear Case

Surface the negatives — force the model out of cheerleader mode and into a red-team posture

That's the whole thing. Three passes. Each one asks the model to play a different role: researcher, relative-value analyst, short-seller. The scoring in step 1 is just a forcing function to make the model commit to a judgment instead of hedging.

Why This Pattern Works (Independent of the Creator)

LLMs default to being agreeable. If you ask "is NVDA a good buy?" in a single prompt, you'll get a balanced, hedged paragraph that's useless for decisions. Splitting analysis into three sequential prompts fixes three specific failure modes:

  • Single-shot bias: Asking "should I buy X" in one prompt lets the model average across perspectives. Forcing it through three separate lenses prevents averaging.
  • Framing inertia: If step 1 frames the stock positively, the model carries that frame forward unless a new prompt resets it. Step 3 (bear case) explicitly flips the frame.
  • Commitment device: A numerical score in step 1 creates an anchor you can compare across tickers. Without it, "looks decent" from one stock and "looks decent" from another are indistinguishable.

This is why the workflow is worth rebuilding even though the reel is a Discord-funnel.

Reconstructable Prompt Templates

These are first-draft scaffolds to refine. They encode the structure the creator describes plus standard equity-research hygiene (explicit scope, structured output, source citations where possible). Treat them as starting points, not finished prompts.

Step 1 Deep Dive

Template — fill in {TICKER}You are an equity research analyst. Produce a deep dive on {TICKER}. Cover these sections in order, using headers: 1. Business overview — what the company actually sells, who buys it, revenue mix. 2. Financial snapshot — latest reported revenue, gross margin, operating margin, net income, FCF, net cash/debt. Flag if trailing vs. most recent quarter. 3. Asymmetry — what is the plausible upside case vs. downside case over the next 12 months? Give a rough % range for each. 4. Catalysts — specific upcoming events (earnings dates, product launches, regulatory decisions, contract cycles) that could move the stock. 5. Score — rate the overall risk/reward on a 1-10 scale with a one-sentence justification. Rules: - If you don't know something recent, say "unknown as of training cutoff" rather than guessing. - Cite filing dates or sources when you quote a number. - Keep each section under 150 words. Total output should fit on one screen.

Step 2 Peer Comparison

Template — fill in {TICKER} and {PEER_LIST}You are a relative-value analyst. Compare {TICKER} to these peers: {PEER_LIST}. Build a table with one row per company and these columns: - Ticker - Market cap (approximate, OK) - Forward P/E or EV/EBITDA (use whichever is standard for the sector) - Revenue growth (last reported YoY %) - Gross margin - One-line qualitative note on business quality After the table, answer three questions: 1. Where does {TICKER} sit on the cheap-to-expensive spectrum relative to peers? 2. Is its multiple justified by its growth/margin profile, or is it a mispricing? 3. Which single peer is the best direct comp, and why? Flag any numbers you're uncertain about. Don't fabricate peer data.

Step 3 Bear Case

Template — fill in {TICKER}You are a short-seller building a bear thesis on {TICKER}. Your job is to find reasons this stock could go down 30% or more. Be adversarial — do not hedge, do not give a "balanced view." Produce: 1. Top 5 specific risks, ranked by how plausible and how damaging each one is. For each: - What breaks the thesis - What evidence would confirm it's happening - Rough probability (low/med/high) and magnitude (small/medium/catastrophic) 2. The single strongest bear argument — the one thing a short-seller would lead with. 3. What's the cheapest way to disprove your own bear case? (i.e. what data point would make you abandon this thesis?) Do not include the bull case. Do not conclude with "however, on balance..." I want the pure bear side.

📝 TODO — pick up here

  • Test all three prompts on a liquid ticker you already have conviction on (e.g. one you own), so you can sanity-check Claude's output against your own view.
  • Decide whether to use Claude with web search enabled vs. pure training-cutoff mode — financials from training are stale, so web search materially changes output quality.
  • Tune the scoring rubric in step 1. A 1-10 scale is vague; consider replacing with explicit criteria (e.g. "score 8+ requires: >20% growth, >60% gross margin, net cash position").
  • Decide on an output format for your own records. These prompts produce prose; you may want JSON so you can track scores over time in a spreadsheet.
  • Decide whether to chain these as three separate conversations (clean context each time) or one long conversation (model carries forward what it learned in step 1). Both have tradeoffs.

Caveats the Reel Doesn't Mention

What's missing from the pitch

Survivorship bias. AAOI and Nebius are named because they worked. The losses from tickers Claude scored 8/10 that went nowhere (or down) aren't in the reel. A 47-second pitch only shows the hits.

LLMs don't have a realtime feed. Without web search enabled, Claude is pricing stocks off training-cutoff data. Any number it cites from memory could be 6+ months stale. This doesn't kill the workflow, but it means step 1 financials need a web-search tool or a live data paste to be reliable.

"Scoring" is opinion, not signal. The score is the model's best guess at risk/reward, not a backtested metric. Two different phrasings of the same prompt can produce scores two points apart on the same stock.

The Discord is the product. The creator's business model is the community and course upsell, not the prompts. The prompts are bait. Once you can rebuild them yourself, the asymmetry flips.

The Meta-Lesson

Every "AI prompt you have to pay for" that's pitched in a short-form video works like this. The creator narrates the workflow on camera because they have to — that's the demo. The narration is usually enough to reverse-engineer what the prompts do. The paywall is for people who don't want to think about it.

Ripping down prompts from reels is its own skill. The pattern to watch for: what is the model being asked to do at each step, and what role is it being asked to play? Once you have that, the specific wording is just polish.
