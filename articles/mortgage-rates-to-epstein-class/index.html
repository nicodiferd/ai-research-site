🏚️ From Your Mortgage Rate to the Epstein Class

A real estate agent gets asked why he's "so political," and answers by tracing one unbroken chain — your mortgage rate to the 10-year Treasury to inflation and war to builder greed to money printing to who actually owns the assets when the system cracks.

TikTok @zacharyloft · ~6 min · Jun 1, 2026

The video is a scripted two-hander. A skeptic asks a real estate agent why he keeps getting political — "you sell houses, stay in your lane." The agent's answer is that you cannot understand why you can't afford a home without following the money all the way up, and that the same machinery that prices you out of the market is the machinery that decides which scandals stay buried. The conceit is provocative, but the spine underneath it is a real and fairly conventional walk through how mortgage rates and home prices are actually set. This page reconstructs that chain link by link.

"The same reason you can't afford a home is the same reason they'd never release the Epstein list." The claim isn't that Epstein sets your mortgage rate. It's that home prices and the rate you pay both terminate in the same small group — banks and billionaires — and that group has no incentive to expose itself.

Link 1: Mortgage rates aren't set by the Fed directly

The skeptic's first instinct is the common one: high mortgage rates come from the Federal Reserve. The agent corrects this. The Fed sets the federal funds rate — the rate at which banks lend to one another overnight (he notes there's actually a small range of policy rates, not a single number). That is not the mortgage rate.

Mortgage rates are set by the bond market. And not the 30-year bond, even though a mortgage is nominally a 30-year product — because most people only stay in a home 7 to 11 years, banks price the loan off the 10-year Treasury. The 10-year is the anchor. Move it, and 30-year mortgage rates move with it.

Link 2: The 10-year moves on inflation — and on fear

So what moves the 10-year? Sentiment about a debt-based, money-printing economy. The agent leans on a point about US exceptionalism: America is the world's reserve currency and can print that same currency, so US bonds behave a little differently from everyone else's. But the core driver is inflation expectations.

His worked example is the cleanest part of the script. Suppose someone offers you a bond paying 5%, but tells you the economy you're holding it in will run 7% inflation. Are you making money or losing money? Losing — your real return is negative. So bond buyers demand higher yields to compensate, which pushes the 10-year up. That, he says, is precisely why yields are elevated: inflation on the horizon, plus a geopolitical risk premium (he names Iran). High 10-year → high mortgage rates. The rate you "can't afford" is a war-and-inflation premium passed straight to your closing table.

Link 3: Who profits from war and inflation?

This is the pivot from finance to politics, and it's a deliberately rhetorical one. The agent asks: who are the beneficiaries of any modern war? His answer is billionaires and bankers — the financiers of conflict. And who, he asks, appears most prominently on the Epstein list? His answer is the same: billionaires and bankers. The bridge he's building is not a smoking gun; it's an overlap-of-class argument. The people who profit from the inflation-and-war premium baked into your mortgage are the same cohort with the most to lose from scrutiny.

Read this as rhetoric, not proof

The Epstein–mortgage link is the video's hook and its weakest empirical leg. It rests on category overlap ("billionaires and bankers benefit from both") rather than any direct causal mechanism. The agent himself keeps having to be dragged back to "but how does this affect prices?" — and the honest finance answers come in the next links, not this one.

Link 4: High prices aren't mainly immigrants and lumber

The skeptic offers the talk-radio explanation: home prices are high because of immigrants and expensive wood. The agent concedes both have some effect — pricier building materials and more people needing shelter genuinely push prices up; denying that would be silly. But he argues neither is a main mover. His evidence is the behavior of the homebuilders themselves through the pandemic cycle.

Link 5: Greedflation — the price went up and never came back down

During the 2021–2022 supply-chain crisis, input costs genuinely spiked, and builders raised prices in response. Then the logistical scares eased and many of those material costs fell. But in those very same years, builders posted record profits — and the price of a finished home never came down to match the falling inputs. The cost-of-building story was used to ratchet the price up; the ratchet had no reverse gear.

"The cost of building the home went up for a bit, they raised the price, and then it never came back down." That gap — between normalized input costs and elevated home prices — is the greedflation the agent says is doing real work.

Link 6: COVID demand + a wall of printed money

Greedflation didn't operate alone. Two other forces stacked on top:

  • A COVID supply freeze. For roughly 18–24 months, few people wanted strangers walking through their homes, so existing inventory came off the market — tightening an already-thin supply.
  • Rates cut to zero. The Fed dropped its rate to ~0%. Crucially, the agent notes this happened when bond rates were already low because inflation wasn't yet a fear — the economy was in a strange, uncertain limbo and a liquidity crunch had been looming. Cheap money met scarce supply.
  • $5T+ printed. The Fed pumped a record amount of liquidity into an economy with almost nothing on the market to buy.

Flush liquidity plus low supply equals a bidding war. Buyers could pay more, so sellers — and builders — could charge more. Builders had the room to undercut resales and win share; instead they chose record-profit years. The agent's read on today: private resales and new-construction sales have begun to freeze, and the question is how long elevated prices can hold before they crack.

Link 7: Epstein, 2008, and toxic assets

To close the loop he opened, the agent reaches back to the financial crisis. He claims Jeffrey Epstein chaired Liquid Funding Limited, a Barbados-based shell vehicle created by Bear Stearns in 2001 (Bear holding ~40% of it), used to offload toxic assets repackaged into CMOs and pushed out to the public market. When those instruments curdled, the Great Financial Crisis began — around the same period Epstein first went to jail. The point isn't a tidy proof; it's that the same financial-engineering class that detonated 2008 is the class that profits at the top of the housing chain today.

Link 8: Real estate as the ultimate wealth machine

Why does this class care so much about housing specifically? Because the top 1% hold a record share of global wealth, and real estate is one of the greatest perpetuators of that wealth — residential, commercial, multifamily high-rises, or raw land. There is permanent value in a finite supply of dirt inside a growing capitalist society, which is why real estate has become one of the most commoditized investments on the planet.

The full cascade, end to end: Inflation + war fear → the 10-year Treasury rises → 30-year mortgage rates rise → you're priced out. Meanwhile: zero rates + $5T printed + frozen supply + builder greedflation → home prices ratchet up and don't fall → assets concentrate in the hands of the class that profits from war, inflation, and financial engineering → that same class has every reason to keep the system (and its scandals) opaque.

Link 9: The recession is the harvest

The bleakest claim is the final one. With sales freezing and prices stretched, the agent asks how much longer until the "Epstein class" engineers something dramatic — a recession — as the mechanism for buying assets on discount from a belittled working class. He frames it as a recurring pattern: post-2001, post-2008, post-1978, post-1974, post-1929. They ring the bell until it cracks the system; when the system cracks, assets go on sale; they buy; they own more; and you rent and are told to be happy about it.

The fourth wall — and the punchline

The agent breaks character ("you must have forgotten this was a skit") and lets the skeptic summarize: rates are high because of war and inflation caused by billionaires and banks; prices are high because of rate manipulation, money printing, and billionaire greedflation. So it's banks and billionaires for all of it. The skeptic's final line — "No dude, it's immigrants" — is the joke: even after walking the entire chain, the convenient scapegoat wins.
