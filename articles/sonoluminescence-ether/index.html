The Experiment: Creating Stars in Water

🔬 Sonoluminescence

Scientists recreated conditions similar to a star by taking a vat of water, placing an air bubble in the center, and passing a sound wave through it. The acoustic pressure causes the bubble to collapse upon itself, creating both heat and light.

This phenomenon—sonoluminescence—has been studied since the 1930s and remains somewhat mysterious. The temperatures reached during bubble collapse can exceed 20,000 Kelvin, hotter than the surface of the sun.

What We Actually Know

📚 Scientific Context

Sonoluminescence is real science. First observed in 1934, it occurs when sound waves cause gas bubbles in liquid to rapidly collapse, emitting brief flashes of light. The exact mechanism is still debated, with theories ranging from blackbody radiation to quantum vacuum effects.

The luminiferous aether was a 19th-century scientific hypothesis that space was filled with a medium for light waves. It was largely abandoned after the Michelson-Morley experiment (1887) and Einstein's special relativity (1905)—though some fringe theories still invoke similar concepts.
