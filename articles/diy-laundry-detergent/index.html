🧺 The $6 DIY Laundry Detergent That Lasts a Year

A three-ingredient "white gold" laundry gel made from grocery-store minerals — the recipe, the ratios, the overnight gel trick, and the caveats the pitch glosses over.

The video's hook is theatrical — "$25 of blue water," a "$60 billion industry," a "900% markup" — but underneath the marketing there's a genuinely simple recipe. The claim: three cheap minerals you can buy at Walmart, mixed in a 5-gallon bucket, produce a concentrated gel that covers roughly 300 loads (about a year for a family of four) for a few dollars. Here is the actual recipe, the ratios, and what the creator does and doesn't tell you.

The argument: a bottle of name-brand detergent is ~90% water and dye, with the real cleaning agent (surfactant) under 3%. You're "paying $25 to ship water across the country in a plastic jug." The DIY version skips the water — you add your own.

The Core Idea: Three Functions, Three Ingredients

The creator's framing is that cleaning clothes only chemically requires three things, and each maps to one cheap ingredient. This is the spine of the whole recipe:

Alkaline catalyst
Washing Soda

Arm & Hammer Super Washing Soda (sodium carbonate). ~$5 for a 3 lb yellow box. Spikes water pH to ~11 to lift proteins and oils off fabric. Not baking soda.

Water softener
Borax

20 Mule Team Borax. ~$5 for a 4 lb box. Neutralizes hard-water minerals so dirt doesn't re-stick to clothes ("cleaning in liquid concrete" without it).

Surfactant
Bar Soap

The soap molecule that grabs grease and rinses it away. The recipe names two different bars — see the caveat below.

The Method

The creator stresses that "the technique matters more than the ingredients." The non-obvious step is the overnight rest that turns the liquid into a gel.

Step 1 Melt the soap

Grate one bar of soap on a cheese grater into a saucepan — aim for about 1 cup of flakes. Add 4 cups of hot water and heat on low, stirring slowly for ~10 minutes until every flake dissolves. Do not let it boil — boiling makes foam that ruins the consistency.

Step 2 Build the bucket

Fill a 5-gallon bucket roughly halfway with the hottest tap water you can get. Pour in the melted soap mixture. Add 1 cup washing soda and 1 cup borax. Stir slowly with a long wooden spoon for ~2 minutes — you'll feel the texture change as it reacts.

Step 3 Top off and rest

Top the bucket off with cold water until full, snap the lid on, and let it sit overnight. Within ~12 hours the whole bucket undergoes a "phase change" into a thick, semi-translucent gel. That gel is the finished detergent.

The ratio, distilled:

1 cup grated soap → melted in 4 cups hot water · 1 cup washing soda · 1 cup borax · topped off in a 5-gallon bucket → rest overnight → gel.

How to Use It

Use about half a cup of gel per load. Scoop it straight into the drum, or into the detergent tray on a front-loader. The creator says it dissolves instantly in any water temperature.

Why it's actually fine for HE machines

The pitch addresses the obvious worry — that homemade soap will wreck a high-efficiency washer. The creator's claim is the opposite: this recipe is naturally low-sudsing, and suds (just air bubbles) are what clog the drainage sensors on HE machines. So a low-suds soap is, by his argument, better-suited to HE drums than foamy synthetic detergent.

The skin-sensitivity angle

Because there are no synthetic dyes, optical brighteners, or fragrance chemicals, the creator claims it won't trigger eczema, baby-skin reactions, or fragrance migraines — and argues even "free & clear" detergents still contain surfactants this recipe supposedly doesn't "need" (a claim worth scrutiny, since bar soap is a surfactant).

Caveats & Things to Verify

Read before you mix

The soap ingredient contradicts itself. The shopping list names castile soap (Dr. Bronner's or Kirk's), but the method section says to grate a bar of Fels-Naptha — a completely different, heavily-degreasing laundry bar. These are not interchangeable in feel or scent. Pick one deliberately; the transcript never reconciles them.

Borax is not universally "safe." The video frames borax as a harmless natural mineral. It's an effective cleaner, but it's an irritant — keep it away from kids/pets, avoid inhaling the powder, and don't assume "natural" means food-safe.

"Cleaner than store-bought" is an assertion, not a test. The claims about HE machines running cleaner, 300 loads, and a full year for a family of four are the creator's figures, not independently measured. Your mileage depends on load size, soil level, and water hardness.

The channel is a ClickBank funnel. The video description leads with affiliate links ("free electricity forever," "self-sufficient on ¼ acre"). The recipe is real and cheap, but treat the surrounding "they tricked you" framing as marketing.

Bottom Line

Stripped of the conspiracy framing, this is a classic homemade laundry gel: grated soap + washing soda + borax, melted and diluted in a bucket, rested into a gel. It is genuinely cheap and genuinely low-suds. The two things to nail before you commit are which bar soap you actually use and your local water hardness — both materially change how well it works.
