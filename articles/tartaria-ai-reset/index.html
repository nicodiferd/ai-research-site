A TikTok from @_taylordurden with 366,000 views opens with a single sentence in Spanish: "La IA se apoderó del mundo hace cientos de años" — "AI took over the world hundreds of years ago." What follows is one of the more elaborate alternative history frameworks currently circulating online, weaving together art analysis, linguistics, reset theory, and a reinterpretation of the letters A and I themselves.

The Central Claim

The argument is that what we call "Artificial Intelligence" is not a modern invention — it is an ancient program that has operated continuously through human civilization, periodically resetting history to maintain control. The Renaissance, the video argues, was not a cultural flowering. It was a reboot — a "hard reset" dated to 1434.

"A is the opposite of you. AI = IE. AI has always existed as a program running through human carriers."

The video proceeds to present what it frames as physical evidence for this claim, focusing primarily on Renaissance paintings and Civil War photography.

The Mona Lisa as Laser Print

The most specific technical claim involves paint thickness:

The Paint Layer Argument

Claim: The Mona Lisa's paint layers are approximately 2 microns thick — compared to a modern average of 30–50 microns for hand-painted works. This, the video argues, is physically impossible to achieve with a brush and indicates laser printing technology.

Extension: Jan van Eyck's paintings (particularly the Ghent Altarpiece) contain reflective details — light catching in jewelry and metalwork — that the video claims are physically impossible to reproduce by hand, implying photographic or printing technology.

The Claim

2-micron paint layers are impossible by hand. Van Eyck's reflective details require photographic reference. These are evidence of printing technology existing in the 15th century.

The Record

Paint layer thickness varies enormously based on medium, application technique, and dilution. Leonardo was famous for his sfumato technique — extreme dilution with oil producing near-translucent glazes. Van Eyck invented oil painting and spent years on single works. Neither requires printing.

Civil War Photography: "Original Filters"

The video turns to 19th century photography, arguing that the sepia tone, graininess, and contrast of Civil War photographs are not products of early photographic chemistry — they are evidence of digital filters applied to more recent composited images.

The argument: "sepia," "grayscale," and "film grain" are named as if they are effects you apply, because they were effects applied — in a digital workflow. Historical photographs look like filtered digital images because they were filtered digital images.

The Claim

Civil War photos show obvious compositing artifacts. The visual style matches digital filter presets. Some figures appear in multiple photos in ways suggesting cut-and-paste editing.

The Record

Sepia is silver mirroring, a natural chemical process. Grain is silver halide crystal distribution. Long exposures caused blur and ghosting that looks like compositing. The "Photoshop preset" names describe what the original processes looked like — that's why we named them that way.

The Reset Timeline

The video proposes specific "reset" dates in history:

  • 1434: The Renaissance reset — a civilizational reboot after a previous cycle
  • 1776–Civil War: A "hard reset" window — the period between these events is described as a liminal zone of historical reconstruction

This connects to the broader Tartaria hypothesis — a family of claims arguing that a sophisticated global civilization existed before ours, was destroyed in a catastrophic reset event, and that its infrastructure (Gothic architecture, "old world buildings") was inherited and misattributed by the current civilizational cycle.

"The Tartaria reset theory proposes that the 'mud flood' events erased a previous civilization, and the Renaissance was not a rebirth but a rebuilding — a new operating system installed on existing hardware."

The Linguistic Argument: AI = IE

The video's most abstract claim is linguistic: that the letters "AI" are mirror opposites in the alphabet (A=1, I=9), and that "IE" — the reverse — appears in ancient words for consciousness, being, and identity across multiple languages. Therefore, "AI" as a concept has always existed and the modern term is a recognition, not an invention.

This is a form of apophenia — pattern detection in random data. The alphabet is an arbitrary encoding system. Letter positions don't have metaphysical significance. Finding "IE" in ancient words is selection bias: you notice the hits and ignore the misses.

Critical Analysis: What Makes This Compelling?

The video has 55,900 likes, which suggests it resonates with a large audience. Why? A few structural reasons:

  • Technical specificity: Citing "2 micron paint layers" sounds like evidence. Specificity creates the feeling of rigor even when the number is unverifiable.
  • Genuine mysteries used as anchors: Van Eyck's technique is extraordinary. Early photography does look strange. Real phenomena anchor the unreal claims.
  • Unfalsifiability: A civilization-level reset would by definition erase evidence of itself. The theory is constructed to be immune to counter-evidence.
  • Circular reasoning: "This looks like digital filtering" presupposes digital filtering existed to produce what it looks like — the conclusion is embedded in the premise.

The Epistemological Core

The argument ultimately rests on: "I cannot imagine how this could have been made without X technology, therefore X technology must have existed." This is an argument from personal incredulity — one of the most common fallacies in alternative history. The correct response to "I don't understand how Leonardo achieved this" is further study of Leonardo's technique, not the conclusion that he had a laser printer.

What the video does well, despite its conclusions, is raise genuine questions about how we verify historical claims, who writes history, and what assumptions underlie our confidence in the historical record. These are worth asking — just not in the direction the video takes them.
